A vs. B — how the two Danger zones differ

Everything else on both screens is byte-identical: same profile card, same Preferences, same read-only trail memory, same About & Legal, same Sign out at the bottom, same Ngā mihi nui footer. The only variable is how Clear my walk history and Delete account are grouped.

Option A — Flat

One "Danger zone" card. Clear and Delete sit as equal siblings, same rust-red text (#b25a48), same pale chevron. Both are destructive, both look destructive, no visual ranking between them.

Where it wins
  • Simpler visual — one card, one label, one mental group
  • Consistent with how iOS system apps handle grouped destructive actions
  • Faster scan: user sees "these two are the scary ones" instantly
Where it costs
  • Delete account and Clear history look equally heavy — a user in a hurry may not perceive Delete as more permanent
  • Relies entirely on the confirmation dialog (and 7-day cool-off) to prevent regret

Option B — Layered

Two cards separated by a gap. Clear my walk history sits alone under the "Danger zone" label in the standard rust. Delete account gets its own card below with a 2 px top border in a deeper red (#c44545), stronger icon tint, and slightly heavier label weight — signalling that this one is a different order of permanence.

Where it wins
  • Delete account visually reads as "the most permanent thing on this whole screen"
  • The card break creates a natural pause — harder to mis-tap on your way past Clear
  • Aligns with real-world consequence: clearing history is undoable-ish (re-record); deleting account is not
Where it costs
  • Adds one more visual break to the tail of the screen — slightly more busy
  • The 2 px red border reintroduces a "shouty" element the rest of the design deliberately avoided
  • Two cards with no shared label needs a moment to parse (why are these two apart?)
Recommendation: If Cairn's users are mostly novices worried about accidentally deleting things, Option B's visual hierarchy earns its cost — it makes the irreversible action clearly heavier than the reversible one, and the small extra visual complexity is worth the mis-tap protection. If the app leans confident and minimalist, Option A keeps the design cleanest and trusts the confirmation dialog to do the safety work. Both are defensible; pick based on how much you want the UI itself (versus the confirm sheet) to carry the "this is serious" signal.
